Inside the bot's brain 🧠

Like Renaissance Technologies, the bot doesn't rely on one big idea. It combines 8 small statistical signals and learns from recent data how much to trust each one. A signal that predicted well lately gets more weight; one that failed gets less, or even flips.

How it works, step by step

  1. 3:00 PM: market closes. A GitHub Actions robot wakes up and downloads every stock's open, high, low, close, volume and average price (VWAP), plus the NEPSE index.
  2. Filter. It throws away mutual funds, bonds, debentures, promoter shares and illiquid stocks (under Rs 50 lakh traded per day) so it can actually buy and sell.
  3. 8 signals. For every remaining stock it measures bounce-back potential, momentum, how stretched the price is, closing pressure, volume, calmness, trend quality and sector strength.
  4. Learn. It checks how well each signal predicted the next 20 days over the last 120 days (only past data, never the future) and sets its trust levels.
  5. Rank & review. Signals × trust = one score per stock. Every 20 trading days (about a month) it reviews the whole portfolio: it buys from the top 10% and sells holdings that fell out of the top 40%. Trading less keeps fees low.
  6. Risk check. Max 10 stocks, max 15% in one stock, max 35% in one sector, less money in stocks when the NEPSE trend is down. Checked daily: an emergency exit at −15% from entry or −20% from the peak.
  7. Next morning. Orders "execute" at that day's VWAP + 0.25% slippage. Orders fail realistically if the stock is locked at the ±10% circuit or doesn't trade.
  8. T+2. Bought shares can't be sold until they settle (2 trading days). Then the cycle repeats every trading day.
VWAP

Volume-weighted average price: the average price everyone paid that day.

T+2 settlement

Shares bought today reach your demat account 2 trading days later. No intraday trading in NEPSE.

Circuit breaker

A stock can move at most ±10% per day. At the limit, nobody may be willing to trade.

Stop-loss

An automatic exit when a trade loses a set amount, to stop small losses becoming big ones.

Drawdown

The fall from the highest value to the lowest point after it.

Sharpe ratio

Return per unit of risk. Above 1 is good; above 2 is excellent.
